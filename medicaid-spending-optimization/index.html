EXECUTIVE OVERVIEW / BUSINESS DECISION & NORTH STAR METRICS

How Our Pharmacy Director Can Recover $3.2M Without Rationing Patient Care

Most regional hospitals run on slim 1% to 3% operating margins. Because insurance plans pay fixed rates for outpatient clinic visits, whenever drug companies raise medication prices, that extra cost comes straight out of the hospital's operating budget. Between 2020 and 2024, national Medicaid outpatient drug spending jumped 46%, rising from $76B to $111B across 4,774 medications.

Our Hospital Pharmacy Director and Chief Medical Officer face a clear business decision: which medications should we switch to lower-cost generics or renegotiate with wholesale suppliers to close our budget gap without disrupting patient treatment? Right now, busy clinic doctors often prescribe familiar brand-name pills out of habit even when identical generics sit on the shelf, and our pharmacy pays up to 72% more when buying uncontracted generic labels from wholesalers.

The Business Decision & Our 3 North Star Metrics: Rather than ordering across-the-board budget cuts that hurt patients, we evaluated every drug in our catalog against three North Star metrics: (1) Cost per Prescription Filled ($408 brand average vs. $22 generic average), (2) Wholesale Supplier Price Spread (72% median price gap across identical generics), and (3) Patient Prescription Access Kept (keeping at least 98% of routine prescriptions untouched).
THE BRAND & SUPPLIER LEAK

Brand-name drugs cost 19x more per prescription than generics ($408 vs. $22), and wholesale prices for identical generics vary by 72%.

* Top 5% of drugs (239 items) eat up 72% ($61M) of our budget.
* Brand drugs take 90% of spending on just 33% of prescriptions.
* 46 older brand drugs raised prices without adding clinical value.
THE FIX: GENERICS & PRICE CAPS

Default e-prescribing to generics for 6 off-patent brands and lock wholesale buying to low-cost suppliers.

* Auto-switch 6 overpriced brands when a generic is on the shelf.
* Cap wholesale generic buying at the lowest 25% supplier price.
* Give doctors a 1-click override for patient allergies.
THE UPSIDE: $3.2M RECOVERED

Recovers $3.2M in annual hospital pharmacy budget with zero reduction in patient care.

* Saves $1.1M from switching 6 brand drugs to generics.
* Saves $2.1M from wholesale generic supplier price locks.
* Keeps 98% of routine hospital prescriptions untouched.
EXECUTIVE QUESTION EVIDENCE-BASED ANSWER & OPERATIONAL ACTION
What did we find? Just 5% of medications (239 drugs) take up 72% ($61M) of our $85M hospital pharmacy budget. Overall, brand-name drugs consume 90% of spending ($77M) despite making up only 33% of prescriptions filled.
Why is this happening? Clinic doctors order familiar brand names like Vyvanse and Concerta even when identical generics cost 50% to 97% less, and pharmacy buyers pay up to 72% more when ordering uncontracted generic labels.
What does it cost us? Our hospital spends $4M a year on off-patent brand pills and inhalers that already have generic replacements, plus $2.1M in avoidable wholesale markups across 1,358 generic medications.

CHAPTER 01 / DATA SCOPE & CAVEATS

Setting a Reliable Baseline: Three Reporting Traps We Fixed for the Pharmacy Team

Real-world healthcare data is messy. Before recommending budget changes to our Pharmacy Director, we audited the 18,511-row Medicaid benchmark file to make sure reporting quirks and missing cells would not skew our savings estimates.

How Unfiltered Drug Files Overstate Spending and Price Spikes

First, the raw government table mixes national total lines with individual manufacturer lines. Adding the table up without separating those rows makes national spending look like $223B instead of the real $111B. Second, under federal patient privacy rules, the government hides prescription counts whenever a drug has fewer than 11 fills in a year (2,657 hidden cells from 2020 to 2023). Treating those hidden cells as zero creates fake growth spikes when a drug crosses 11 prescriptions the following year.

"Removing duplicate summary lines and separating mixed package sizes cleared out $2B in false price gaps, giving our hospital a reliable $4M target brand list ($5B nationally)."
DATA QUALITY & CAVEATS CHECK | 4,774 ACTIVE MEDICATIONS

Caveats & Scope Assumptions for Our Hospital Model

Because national Medicaid files report pre-rebate spending and mix everyday pills with long-acting clinic injections, we separated 1,738 mixed-package and rare specialty records ($9B national spend) onto a separate clinical watchlist. Our $85M hospital budget model scales the remaining 4,774 verified outpatient medications to a regional hospital system and applies conservative 60% to 80% Year-1 doctor adoption rates.

CHAPTER 02 / BUDGET BREAKDOWN

Top 5% of Medications Take Up 72% ($61M) of Our Hospital Pharmacy Budget

Outpatient pharmacy spending is heavily concentrated in a small group of expensive medications. Just 165 drugs (3% of our catalog) drive 65% ($55M) of our $85M hospital pharmacy budget. Expanding to the top 5% of medications (239 drugs) accounts for 72% ($61M) of all spending.

Across the entire catalog, brand-name drugs eat up 90% of our budget ($77M hospital / $100B national) while making up only 33% of prescriptions filled (246M fills), averaging $408 per prescription. Meanwhile, everyday generic pills deliver 67% of all prescriptions filled (500M fills) for just 10% of the budget ($8M hospital / $11B national) at an average cost of $22 per fill. That is a 19x cost difference between brands and generics.

How Everyday Generics Keep Care Affordable

Roughly 90% of our catalog consists of 4,296 routine generic medications that take up only 15% of the outpatient pharmacy budget. These everyday pills treat high blood pressure, cholesterol, and routine infections for about $22 per prescription. Spending pharmacist time auditing these inexpensive generics does not move the hospital budget.

Where Brand-Name Habits Drain the Budget

Budget pressure comes from 25 high-cost brand medications that alone take up 32% ($27M) of hospital spending, led by Humira, Ozempic, Biktarvy, Jardiance, and Trulicity. In addition, doctors frequently prescribe older brand names like Vyvanse ($13 per pill vs. $6 generic) and Concerta ($13 per pill vs. under $1 generic) out of habit, even though 10 or more suppliers make the exact same generic pill.

"When a brand-name medication costs 19x more than an average generic ($408 vs. $22 per fill), waiting months for a partial manufacturer rebate still leaves the hospital losing money. Switching to generics upfront saves far more."

CHAPTER 03 / PRICE VS VOLUME

Is Spending Rising Because We Treat More Patients, or Because Drug Prices Jumped?

Looking only at total spending on a drug can be misleading. If our outpatient clinics spend more on diabetes or heart medications this year because we are treating 300 more patients, that is healthy patient care. It only becomes a purchasing problem when a drug company raises the price per dose on the exact same number of patients.

Across 3,762 medications with complete 5-year histories, we separated spending changes into two simple parts: (1) Price Changes (when a manufacturer charges more for the exact same dose) and (2) Volume Changes (when doctors treat more patients).

Protecting Growing Patient Care vs. Stopping Price Gouging

Our breakdown showed that 88 of the top 100 fastest-growing medications grew because more patients received care, not because of price hikes. For example, Ozempic prescriptions jumped more than 10x (from 322K to over 3M fills) while its cost per unit actually dropped 26%. Jardiance prescriptions grew more than 3x (+257%) as heart and diabetes guidelines expanded. The hospital should protect access to those treatments.

In contrast, we found a target group of 46 high-spend medications where drug companies hiked prices without adding new benefits. For example, Trulicity prices jumped 107%, Xifaxan spending growth was 74% price-driven, Emflaza was 66% price-driven, and Vyvanse raised unit prices 37% even as prescription volume fell 15%.

"88 of the top 100 growing drugs expanded because more patients got treated. Separating volume from price lets us protect growing patient care while targeting the 46 drugs that simply raised prices."
PRICE VS. VOLUME AUDIT
THREE GROUPS IN OUR PRICE VS. VOLUME GRID
1. Stable Generic Baseline (1,520 drugs): Steady or falling prices ($47 to $181 per fill). Keeps everyday care affordable with no changes needed.

2. Patient Growth Group (88 of Top 100): Spending rose because more patients needed care while unit prices stayed flat or fell (such as Ozempic unit cost down 26%). Protect patient access.

3. Price-Spike Target Group (46 drugs): Manufacturers raised prices sharply (+37% to +107%) on older brands. Primary targets for generic switching.
OPTION A: BLANKET BUDGET CAPS

Putting strict limits across all high-spend drugs penalizes diabetes and heart patients whose medication prices are actually flat or falling. Disrupts 12% of chronic care.

OPTION B: SWITCH ONLY PRICE-SPIKE BRANDS

Targeting only the 46 price-spike drugs and 6 off-patent brands recovers $3.2M while keeping routine prescription changes under 2% (well below our 3% hospital limit).

CHAPTER 04 / SAVINGS PLAN

Hospital Pharmacy Savings Plan: Recovering $3.2M Every Year

To measure the exact dollar impact on our $85M hospital outpatient pharmacy budget, we tested two practical purchasing changes that require zero reduction in patient care.

First, switching 6 off-patent brand pills and inhalers to FDA-approved generics saves $1.1M ($1,100K) a year. Second, across 1,358 generic medications made by multiple suppliers, wholesale prices for the exact same pill vary by a median of 72%. Locking our hospital wholesaler account to buy from the lowest-priced 25% of suppliers saves an additional $2.1M ($2,100K) a year, bringing total annual savings to $3.2M ($3,200K).
