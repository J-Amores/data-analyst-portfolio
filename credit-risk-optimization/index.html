EXECUTIVE OVERVIEW / BUSINESS DECISION & NORTH STAR METRICS

How Our Underwriting Team Can Recover $244.2M Without Choking Off Loan Growth

Between 2007 and 2018, LendingClub funded over 2.26 million consumer loans totaling $34B. As the platform pushed for faster growth, it approved hundreds of thousands of higher-risk borrowers in Grades D through G at 18% to 29% interest rates. On paper, those high interest rates looked profitable, and basic company dashboards showed a safe 12% default rate because 912,569 newer loans were still making monthly payments.

Our Chief Risk Officer and Head of Underwriting face a practical business decision: which high-risk loan applications should we stop approving or reprice so we protect our profit margins without shutting down our lending volume? When we separated out the unfinished active loans and looked at the 1.35M completed loans ($19.4B), we found that 1 in 5 borrowers actually defaulted (20%), and unpaid write-offs in Grades D through G were eating up the profits earned from safer borrowers.

The Business Decision & Our 3 North Star Metrics: Rather than banning all subprime loans and losing 28% of our business, we evaluated every borrower group against three North Star guardrails: (1) Net Cash Return After Losses (LAROC) (keeping portfolio return above our +4.0% profit hurdle), (2) Completed Default Rate (bringing overall defaults down below 19.3%), and (3) Lending Volume Kept (keeping at least 80% of our lending volume intact).
THE SUBPRIME & PRICING LEAK

Subprime Grades D to G receive 28% of our lending money ($5.5B) but cause 51% of all unpaid write-offs ($1.4B).

* Unfiltered reports hid risk at 12% vs. 20% true default rate.
* Net return drops below our +4.0% goal at Subgrade C4 (+3.4%).
* Subgrades E5 to G5 lose money (-1.3% down to -9.8% return).
THE FIX: TARGETED CUTOFFS & RATES

Decline only subprime applicants with both high monthly debt and maxed-out cards, and adjust Subgrade C4 pricing.

* Auto-decline Grades D-G when DTI > 25% and Card Usage > 65%.
* Add +1.5% (+150 bps) interest rate to Subgrade C4 loans.
* Cap 5-year terms at 680+ FICO and verify small business taxes.
THE UPSIDE: +$244.2M RECOVERED

Recovers $244.2M in combined value while keeping 96% of our lending volume ($18.6B) moving smoothly.

* Saves +$230.0M in unpaid losses (18,548 defaults prevented).
* Adds +$14.2M in interest income across 74,422 C4 loans.
* Lifts portfolio net return from +4.3% to +4.4% (+15 bps).
Executive Question Analyst Finding & Underwriting Action
What did we find? Separating out 912,569 unfinished active loans showed our true completed default rate is 20% (268,599 defaults across 1.35M loans), not 12%. Subprime Grades D to G take up 28% of lending ($5.5B) but drive 51% of all unpaid losses ($1.4B).
Why is this happening? Charging 20% to 29% interest does not make up for 30% to 50% of borrowers defaulting early. Losses spike when subprime borrowers spend over 25% of their monthly income on debt while their credit cards are over 65% full (39% default rate).
What does it cost us? Subprime write-offs drain $1.4B in unpaid principal, borderline Subgrade C4 loans miss our +4.0% profit hurdle (+3.4% return), 5-year loans default at double the rate of 3-year loans (32% vs. 16%), and unsecured small business loans lose money (-0.6% return).
What should we change? Turn on an automated decline rule for Grade D-G applicants with DTI > 25% and Card Usage > 65% (Phase 1: +$230.0M saved), raise Subgrade C4 rates by +1.5% and cap 5-year terms (Phase 2: +$14.2M), and require tax returns for small business loans (Phase 3).
OVERVIEW TAKEAWAY
Subprime Grades D to G cause over half of our unpaid loan losses ($1.4B). Blocking only the highest-stress subprime group and raising Subgrade C4 rates by 1.5% recovers $244.2M while keeping 96% of our lending volume untouched.
CHAPTER 01 / DATA SCOPE & CAVEATS

Setting a Reliable Baseline: Why Standard Reports Underestimated Default Risk by 1.7x

When standard business reporting evaluated the historical database, it calculated an overall default rate of 12% (268,648 defaults out of 2.26 million total notes). This apparent stability led executives and institutional lenders to believe credit risk was safely contained.

Why Borrowers Default Later in Loan Life

In consumer credit, loans rarely default in their first few months. Instead, borrowers prioritize payments to protect their newly opened credit lines. Default rates peak sharply between months 12 and 24, before tapering off as mature balances are paid down.

Because the platform expanded lending rapidly in its later years, 912,569 loans ($14.6B) were active accounts still in repayment. With a median age under 14 months, these active loans had simply not been open long enough to reach their peak default period. Mixing unfinished accounts into the portfolio total artificially watered down the default rate by 40%.

"Looking only at completed loans revealed that 1 in 5 borrowers actually defaulted (20%). Standard reporting methods underestimated real portfolio risk by 1.7 times."
Historical Portfolio Audit Finding

Separating Completed and Active Loans

To establish a reliable baseline for underwriting updates, we divided the portfolio into two clean groups:

1. Completed Study Cohort (1,345,350 loans / $19.4B): Loans that reached their final outcome (either fully paid or written off as default). This group provides ground-truth default numbers.

2. Active Surveillance Cohort (912,569 loans / $14.6B): Open accounts still making monthly payments, tracked for servicing support and early payment assistance rather than new underwriting rules.

Our 3 Core Underwriting Guardrails
Before evaluating policy updates, our credit committee established three non-negotiable operational boundaries:

1. Profit Hurdle: Keep net cash return on capital (LAROC) above +4.0% after absorbing all write-offs.

2. Default Ceiling: Bring portfolio-wide completed default rate below 19.3%.

3. Volume Guardrail: Keep at least 80% ($15.5B+) in total lending volume to maintain commercial scale and marketplace liquidity.

CHAPTER 02 / LOSS BREAKDOWN

The Subprime Subsidy: Grades D to G Get 28% of Lending Money but Cause 51% of Losses

LendingClub segmented borrowers into seven risk tiers from Grade A (highest credit quality) down to Grade G (deep subprime). As the platform expanded, origination targets pushed capital heavily into lower-grade tiers to capture higher interest rates (18% to 29% APR). However, our audit revealed an extreme imbalance between dollars lent and unpaid write-offs.

While subprime loans (Grades D through G) took up 28% of platform lending ($5.5B across 335,813 loans), they caused 51% of all unpaid write-offs ($1.4B out of $2.7B total). In practical terms, subprime losses exceeded proportional lending by an alarming 1.8 times.

How Safer Borrowers Subsidized Subprime Deficits

A sustainable lending business generates positive net profit across every credit tier. In our portfolio, however, safer borrowers were effectively paying for deep subprime losses:

Borrowers in Grades A through C generated +$857M in cumulative net profit, funding over 100% of total platform earnings. Grade A and B borrowers repaid consistently (default rates between 6% and 13%), delivering +$654M in net profit despite conservative interest rates. Grade C added another +$203M.

Losing Principal in Grades F and G

In sharp contrast, the lowest credit tiers suffered from default rates that completely wiped out high contractual interest rates. Grade F borrowers defaulted at 45.2%, while Grade G borrowers defaulted at 50% (essentially a coin flip).

Combined, Grades F and G produced an outright cash deficit (-$32M net cash loss). Charging 25% to 29% APR cannot make up for half of all borrowers failing to repay their loans. Continuing to approve unconstrained subprime loans was actively destroying capital.

The High-Interest Fallacy
Risk-based pricing has real limits. In unsecured consumer credit, pricing loans above 25% APR attracts borrowers in severe financial distress who accept high rates simply because they cannot get credit anywhere else. Rate increases alone cannot cure subprime default losses.
Where Margin Leakage Begins: Subgrade C4 (+3.4% return) & E5 (-1.3% loss)

While Grade C overall earns a +4.2% net return, portfolio profitability drops below our +4.0% profit goal starting specifically at Subgrade C4 (+3.4% net return across 74,422 loans), slides further across Grade D, and turns negative at Subgrade E5 (-1.3% net return, dropping to -9.8% in G5).

Remedy: Raising interest rates by +1.5% (+150 bps) on Subgrade C4 restores our +4.0% hurdle and adds +$14.2M in annual interest revenue.
Duration & Purpose Leakage: 5-Year Loans (32% default vs. 16% on 3-year) & Small Business Loans (30% default, -0.6% loss)

Beyond credit grades, two loan characteristics drain substantial margin:

1. 5-Year (60-Month) Loans: Default at 32% (double the 16% rate on 36-month loans), cutting net return in half (+2.6% vs. +5.1%).
2. Unsecured Small Business Loans ($240.1M book): Hit a 30% default rate and lose money overall (-0.6% net return).

Remedy: Require a 680+ credit score for 5-year loans and verify 2 years of IRS tax returns before funding small business loans.
CHAPTER 02 KEY TAKEAWAY
Grades D through G represent an unsustainable 1.8x loss imbalance, causing over half of all unpaid write-offs ($1.4B). Yet a blanket ban on all subprime loans would wipe out $5.5B in lending, violating our volume goal. We need targeted rules that separate performing subprime borrowers from high-risk defaults.
CHAPTER 03 / BORROWER STRESS GRID

Combining Monthly Debt Payments and Credit Card Usage to Find Our Cutoff

To cut subprime losses without turning away healthy lending volume, we investigated the underlying financial pressures that cause borrowers to default. Traditional underwriting models often look at only one metric at a time, rejecting applicants based strictly on debt-to-income (DTI) or strictly on credit card utilization. Our findings show why single-variable screening fails.

Why Single-Variable Rules Reject the Wrong Borrowers

Screening on monthly debt alone turns away high-earning professionals with substantial mortgages or student loans who comfortably make payments from strong monthly income. On the other hand, screening on credit card usage alone penalizes disciplined prime borrowers who use cards for points and pay balances in full each month.

Single-variable rules are blunt: they reject creditworthy borrowers, causing high commercial friction, while missing high-risk borrowers who sit just below individual thresholds.

The Combined Effect of Debt Payments and Maxed-Out Cards

Consumer default is fundamentally a cash crunch. We tested two key financial dimensions together:

1. Monthly Debt Ratio (DTI): Measures what percentage of a borrower's gross monthly income goes toward existing debt payments. A high ratio leaves very little spare cash each month.

2. Credit Card Usage (Utilization): Measures how much of a borrower's credit limit is currently drawn. High usage shows the borrower has already tapped their emergency credit backup.

When a borrower experiences one stressor alone, they usually manage: a borrower with high monthly debt can use open credit cards to cover an unexpected expense, while a borrower with full credit cards can use spare cash from income to pay them down.

However, when both variables cross critical limits together (DTI > 25% AND Card Usage > 65%), the borrower has no cushion left. They have neither monthly spare cash nor available credit cards. Any unexpected bill (a car repair or medical emergency) leaves them unable to make payments, triggering an immediate loan default.

What the 4x4 Risk Grid Shows

Across the 1.35 million completed loans, default rates climb steadily as both debt payments and credit card usage rise:

* Low-Risk Baseline (DTI ≤ 15% and Card Usage ≤ 40%): Default rate of only 14% across 213,691 loans. These borrowers have both comfortable monthly cash flow and ample open credit.

* Moderate-Risk Zone: As either metric increases independently, default rates rise moderately into the 16% to 23% range.

* High-Stress Group (DTI > 25% and Card Usage > 65%): Default rates jump to 28% across 112,246 total loans. When we look specifically at subprime borrowers (Grades D through G) inside this group, the default rate surges to 39% (47,705 loans).

Option A: Blanket Ban on All Subprime (Grades D-G)

Eliminates all $5.5B in subprime lending (28% of the platform loan volume).

Result: FAILS volume goal (only 72% kept vs. 80% minimum floor) and turns away $4.1B in good, performing loans.
Option B: Targeted Cutoff (DTI > 25% AND Card Usage > 65%)

Declines only the 47,705 subprime applications in the high-stress group defaulting at 39%.

Result: PASSES volume goal (96% kept / $18.6B preserved) while saving +$230.0M in net unpaid losses.
CHAPTER 03 KEY TAKEAWAY
Default risk spikes when high monthly debt payments combine with maxed-out credit cards. By requiring both DTI > 25% and card usage > 65%, we isolate a specific subprime borrower group defaulting at 39%, enabling targeted loss reduction without broad commercial damage.

How the $230.0M in Net Savings Adds Up

The recovery waterfall walks through three clear steps:

1. Current Subprime Losses ($1,404.1M): The baseline unpaid write-offs caused by Grades D through G across 335,813 completed loans.

2. Phase 1 Automated Decline Savings (-$230.0M net): Declining the 47,705 high-stress subprime applications prevents 18,548 defaults, eliminating $254.1M in gross unpaid write-offs. After subtracting $24.1M in forgone interest and servicing margin from the performing loans in that group, the net savings is $230.0M.

3. Remaining Subprime Losses ($1,174.1M): With the decline rule in place, subprime write-offs drop by 16%, while the platform keeps $18.6B (96%) of total lending dollars.

Protecting Origination Scale

The core advantage of this targeted rule is its precision: it stops 18,548 bad loans while declining only 3.6% of loan applications. Rather than shrinking our platform, we remove only the most concentrated risk.

Across the total loan book, net return on capital (LAROC) rises from +4.3% to +4.4% (+15 bps), safely above our +4.0% profit goal. At the same time, the portfolio default rate drops to 19.0%, beating our 19.3% default ceiling.

Our servicing team sends automated payment-plan options to the 38,114 active borrowers ($588.5M) as soon as they hit 16 days past due.
