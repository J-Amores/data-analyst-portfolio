Case Study 05 · US YouTube trending board

One-hit or repeat — what separates a small channel that trends twice?

Smaller channels on the US trending board, meaning channels whose median trending video draws under 354,264 views: 3,489 of 6,250 videos (55.8%), 1,385 of 2,099 channels (66.0%). Engagement is likes + comments per hundred views, taken on each video's first day on the board, back-catalogue excluded (101 videos). 205 days, 2017-11-14 → 2018-06-14.

You came in thinking trending is luck and that nothing you touch moves it. Here is the part of that this study can settle. If it were luck, the channels that went on to trend again would have looked no different beforehand. They did not.

Channels that trended more than once were already ahead on their very first trending video, before any repeating had happened. The typical one scored 4.84% against 3.05%, at least 1.3 and possibly as much as 2.2 points more, on 445 channels against 899. At your size a point is about 1,190 reactions an upload, so the gap between the two groups is worth roughly 2,135 reactions on a typical video: at least 1,567, possibly 2,624.

The short version

  • The gap is real, and it is not a to-do list. Five things you own were tested against it. Four tell the two groups apart and the fifth, tag count, does too: 17 against 11. Not one is a switch you can flip — 94.76% of one-hit channels already write a description, 96.79% already leave comments on, and inside a single channel the publish-slot difference all but disappears.
  • The difference is not in your upload box. Of the roughly 9,810 reactions an upload that separate the bottom tenth of videos from the top tenth, all three settings you own account for about 161. The other 9,648 sit elsewhere. That count and the 3.67% in the tiles above are two rulers on the same finding — variance explained against reactions moved — and one is not the other multiplied out.
  • It is not category either, even though category is the biggest thing in this file. Category accounts for 21.02% of the variance between videos, six times those three settings. But it is not what separates the two groups of channels: hold the mix of categories common across both and 74.4% of the gap between them is still there.
  • What you can hand a sponsor is a position, not an average. A quarter of smaller-channel videos come in under 1.72%, half under 3.53%, three quarters under 6.02%. A channel whose typical video runs above 6.02% is in the top quarter of a board where every video already trended, and that is a sentence a brand can price.
  • On timing, there was only enough data to test six categories: Comedy, Entertainment, Howto & Style, Music, News & Politics and People & Blogs — 2,488 of 3,489 smaller-channel videos (71.3%). A gap turned up in two, 1,066 videos (30.6%). The other four came back empty — a measured no in News & Politics, too few videos to tell in Comedy, Howto & Style and Music. In the other ten (1,001 videos, 28.7%) there is no priced timing move for you here.
  • Price any trade at the ceiling you set when this was scoped: 25,000 views for one point of engagement. At a ceiling of 20,000 instead, the one move here that clears it would not.
  • Where the difference does sit, this file cannot see inside. It travels with the channel rather than the upload, and there is no subscriber count, watch time or click-through here to look further. That is the edge of this study, not a hedge. Which way the arrow points cannot be settled either: a channel may trend again because of how it packages, or package that way because it already had an audience, and nothing here separates those.

Six questions follow, in the order they were asked. Each one gets a chart and a straight answer, and the recommendations are at the end.

What is a typical rate at my size?

The typical smaller-channel video scores 3.53%, and hardly any video sits at the typical

The number a sponsor asks for first is the one this study settles most cleanly.

  • Being small is not the problem. Your typical trending video draws 119,028 views and converts 3.53% into a like or a comment. The whole board, giants included, sits at 3.73% on 279,895 views — close enough that the usual assumption, that a small channel is punished on engagement, does not survive contact with the data.
  • The spread is the problem. The middle 80% of smaller-channel videos runs from 0.88% to 9.12%, a 10.4-fold range. On the same 119,028 views that is about 1,045 reactions at the bottom tenth against 10,855 at the top — a range worth roughly 9,810 likes and comments an upload.
  • So 3.53% is a location, not a standard any single video is expected to hit.
  • Hand a brand a position, not an average. A quarter of smaller-channel videos come in under 1.72%, half under 3.53%, three quarters under 6.02%, the top tenth above 9.12%. A channel whose typical video runs above 6.02% is in the top quarter of a board where every video already trended. Give the number, say which basis it was measured on, say where it sits.
  • It is not just categories mixed together. Cooking videos and news clips in one bucket would explain the spread, except that inside a single category 78.6% of it is still there. Whatever produces it is not what the video is about.

Two videos score a genuine 0.0%: nobody liked and nobody commented, on 2,197 and 2,259 views. Both are real and kept in every figure. And the level depends on when you take it — the same videos read 3.74% on their first board day against 2.83% on their last, a quarter lower, while views nearly double. The ordering barely changes, which is what makes first-day figures comparable at all, but a rate quoted on one basis is not the rate on the other. Say which you mean when a sponsor asks.

Does category separate the two groups?

Category is the biggest thing in this file, and it is not what separates the two groups

Category accounts for about 21% of the video-to-video spread — six times all three settings you own together, and the largest measured effect in the study. It sits on a decision you own, so be exact about what it buys.

  • It buys a location. Music leads at 7.09% (6.53–7.60), Sports trails at 1.48% (1.26–1.80), and that distance is real.
  • It does not buy an order to work down. Music is the only category whose position holds in every re-draw; seven of the fourteen rankable categories could sit four or more places higher or lower; the bottom two cannot be told apart at all, with News & Politics 0.33 points above Sports in a range from 0.18 lower to 0.92 higher. A ranked table of sixteen would be mostly decoration.
  • What it does do is locate your own baseline. Your category's median against the board-wide 3.73% is what your rate should be judged against, not the top of the table. Where two ranges overlap, the order between them is not information, however confidently a table prints it.
  • The constraint here is yours, not ours. At scoping you set the slate as format tilt only — changes inside your category, not a switch to another. That is why nothing here reads "make more of this, drop that".
  • It is also not what you would want. Category mix does separate the two groups, in both directions: repeat channels are more concentrated in Howto & Style by 6.48 points, Entertainment by 5.88, News & Politics by 5.30 and Education by 3.08, and less concentrated in People & Blogs by 6.36. Every one of those ranges is wide and on any one of them we cannot tell you where in the range you are, so there is no category to tilt toward.
  • And it would not close the gap. Holding the mix common across the two groups leaves 74.4% of the first-video gap standing — a free switch of category would leave three quarters of the difference where it was.
  • Reach is a different ranking again. Category order by views and by engagement line up at only 0.37 out of 1, and on fourteen categories this data cannot rule out that they are unrelated — Sports is 6th on views and 14th on engagement. Eyes do not bring reactions, which is why the trade later is priced in views per point.

Categories do not lose videos to the comments-off rule evenly. News & Politics loses 6.37% of its videos that way against Music's 0.63%, roughly a tenfold difference, so its 1.82% is measured only on the News & Politics videos that kept reactions switched on. That belongs beside any News & Politics figure, including its place near the bottom of this table.

Does the ranking hold at my size?

The giants' ranking holds at your size: one category in eleven behaves differently, and it is Music

The reasonable objection is that this is a board of Music and Entertainment giants, and a channel at your scale plays a different game. It is testable, and it does not hold.

  • The two boards rank alike. Ranked separately, the smaller-channel and larger-channel boards line up at 0.88 out of 1 across the eleven categories with enough videos on both sides, and the smaller-channel rate sits 0.20 points below the all-videos figure — too small for this data to separate from no gap at all.
  • Exactly one category really differs by scale: Music, where smaller channels run 2.19 points lower (1.24 to 3.32). Comparing eleven categories at once, one or two will look unusual by chance; allow for that and nine of the other ten sit within about a point of zero, ranges covering zero. Music is also where a "copy the big channels" instinct would mislead you most, and nobody flagged it in advance.
  • Who is in this group, because the comparison rests on it: a channel whose own median trending video draws under 354,264 views. That ceiling sits well above the group's centre — half its videos draw under 119,028, a quarter under 41,003, a tenth under 11,400. It runs from eleven thousand views up to a third of a million and holds two thirds of the channels here.
  • The real hole, and it is not a technicality: every member trended at least once. If you have never trended, this board cannot place you. Nothing here describes the step onto the board — only what separates channels already on it.

The larger-channel arm of News & Politics is 61 videos from 10 channels, 47 of them (77%) from a single channel, which is why its range is wide and lopsided and why it does not survive the correction. Gaming clears the 30-video floor at exactly 30 videos, so its third place carries almost no information and should not be read as a rank.

Do the settings I own move it?

All three settings you own account for about 161 reactions of a 9,810-reaction spread

This is where a packaging playbook would normally start, and where this one stops. Publish slot, tag count and comments-on are the three things here a creator decides on every upload.

  • Together they account for at most 3.67% of the video-to-video spread (2.56–6.63%), leaving at least 96.33% outside them. "At least" is the honest word: 3.67% is an upper bound, so the 96.33% can only be higher.
  • In the units you actually count, that is about 161 reactions. The 3.67% is a share of the variance, the statistician's unit and not yours. Give every video the median of its own publish-slot-and-tag-band group and see what spread survives: the distance from the bottom tenth to the top tenth is about 9,810 likes and comments an upload, and fixing all three settings narrows it to 9,648. The two are different rulers on one finding, and neither is the other multiplied out — 3.67% is variance explained, 161 is what the range actually moved, and 98.36% of the range still stands.
  • One at a time, two of the three look real. Afternoon videos run 1.47 points above evening ones (0.79–1.96), worth 0.7% of the spread; tagged videos run 2.11 above untagged (1.15–2.59), worth 1.5%. A playbook could be written off those two sentences.
  • It would be wrong, because videos come attached to channels. Take the 84 channels that published in both blocks and compare each against itself: the gap falls to 0.08 points (0.11 lower to 0.52 higher), a 94.5% shrinkage. Nor is a thinner slice doing the work — on those same 84 channels, compared the ordinary way, the gap is fully present at 1.17 points. Hold the channel fixed and the slot stops telling you anything.
  • Category, by contrast, accounts for 21.02% of the same spread — six times the three settings combined. Engagement at this scale looks more like a property of the channel than of the upload, and three separate questions in this study land on that arithmetic from three directions.
  • That is a bound, not an algorithm-shrug, and a bound is usable: the effort spent tuning slots and tag counts is being spent on at most 3.67% of the spread, and you can stop spending it.

The comments-on line rests on 49 smaller-channel videos whose owners turned comments off. That is a choice, not an accident, so the comparison cannot separate the choice from whatever preceded it. It is also the one setting with no engagement rate to compare: a video with comments disabled has no comment count, so it is measured on the like-to-dislike ratio instead.

Is there a timing move worth its price?

Two of the six testable categories show an afternoon gap, and neither survives on weekday publishes alone

This file does not tell you to move your publish slot. Here is how far it got, because you own the calendar and are entitled to the working.

  • There is no "publish at 4pm" here. A move gets priced only where both the afternoon block (14:00–18:59 ET) and the evening block (19:00–23:59 ET) hold at least 30 smaller-channel videos — true in six categories, 2,488 of 3,489 videos (71.3%). A gap turned up in two: 1,066 videos (30.6%), held by 441 channels. Ten categories, 1,001 videos (28.7%), could not be priced at all, and they get a straight answer rather than a hint: there is no timing move for you in this file.
  • Entertainment — priced, and inside your rule. At least 0.3 points higher in the afternoon and possibly 1.9, on 182 afternoon against 96 evening videos, and we cannot tell you where in that range you are. It costs 20.7% of the views, 22,491 fewer per upload, pricing the trade at 22,797 views per point — inside your 25,000 ceiling.
  • People & Blogs — too wide to act on. At least 0.1 points higher and possibly 5.2, on 87 and 54 videos, and again we cannot tell you where in that range you are. 0.1 is close to nothing and 5.2 would be the largest effect in the document: not one finding with a margin, two different worlds. It costs nothing, since views run 69.1% higher in the afternoon too.
  • News & Politics — a measured no, 0.4 points lower to 0.5 higher, too small to change any sponsor conversation.
  • Comedy, Howto & Style, Music — could not tell, too few videos to say. That is a different instruction from a measured no, and this report keeps them apart.
  • The clock may not be what is being measured. Publish block and day of week travel together: on Monday-to-Friday publishes Entertainment's gap falls from 0.99 to 0.56 and People & Blogs' from 2.90 to 1.57, both ranges then covering zero, and both are larger on weekends. This may be about weekends rather than afternoons, and this data cannot separate them.
  • You cannot settle it yourself. Detecting Entertainment's gap would take about 200 uploads in each block, about 80 for People & Blogs — at one upload a week, years of your calendar. That is why no timing move appears in the recommendations.
  • Use it as a filter on everyone else's timing advice: before you act on it, make sure it was measured in your category at your size. If yours is one of the ten categories that could not be tested (1,001 of 3,489 videos, 28.7%), hold your calendar.
  • One cost sits outside this data, and it is priced with the rules at the end rather than here: moving your block costs you time, not views, and no column in this file can value it.

The detected pair is finely balanced: People & Blogs is detected on a lower bound of 0.075 points, a hair above nothing, on 290 videos (8.31% of 3,489). The other side of that line and "found something" would read Entertainment alone — 776 videos, 22.24%. Separately, 63.5% of smaller-channel videos publish in neither block, so the afternoon-versus-evening pair speaks to 1,273 videos (36.5%) — a different group from the 71.3% that could be tested at all, and not to be read as the same number.

That 63.5% is 2,216 videos, and it is the biggest thing here left unpriced: the majority of your uploads go out in neither block, and publishing outside both is also the only publish-slot level that told the two groups of channels apart. This study never priced it. If you commission one more piece of work off the back of this, that is the one to commission.

What actually separated the two groups?

Channels that trended twice were already ahead on their very first trending video

Of the 1,385 smaller channels on this board, 450 trended more than once and 935 did it exactly once.

  • The comparison is built to be fair. Everything the repeat channels published against the one-hit channels' single video would answer itself, because trending repeatedly is how you get more videos into it. So each repeat channel is represented by its first trending video only, taken before any repeating had happened, and matched against the one-hit channels' only video. Both sides are one video per channel.
  • On that footing the two groups differ, and differed from the start. Repeat channels' first videos ran at 4.84% engagement against 3.05%: at least 1.3 points and possibly as much as 2.2, on 445 channels against 899.
  • What that is worth to you. At the smaller-channel median of 119,028 views a point is about 1,190 likes and comments per upload, so about 2,135 reactions an upload sit on the far side of that difference, at least 1,567 and possibly 2,624. That is what the gap between the two groups is worth — not a gain anyone here is promising you.
  • That reaction range is narrower than the truth, and here is why. It moves with the engagement gap alone. The typical-views figure it converts through is itself an estimate with its own range, 104,194 to 132,898 views, and that width is not in the 1,567 to 2,624. Read the reaction figures as the gap priced at a typical channel, not as the full spread.

It survives the checks that usually kill a finding like this.

  • Not category mix. Hold the mix common across both groups and 74.4% of the gap remains, still clear of zero.
  • Not one slice of the data. 16 of 16 cuts point the same way — inside each publish block, on a stricter definition of "repeat", excluding the fifteen largest channels, and at four different scale thresholds. 11 of the 16 exclude zero on their own.
  • Not the window's edge. Drop the 116 videos already on the board on opening day and the gap widens to 1.93 points.

And then the recommendation set empties, on this study's own standing rule that a true finding you cannot act on does not get to masquerade as an action. Five things a creator owns could be tested against this comparison. Four of them tell the two groups apart, and tag count, the fifth, does too. Not one is a switch:

  • Tag count. Repeat channels' first videos carry 17 tags against 11, a difference of at least 4 and possibly 8 — the most concrete thing here and the one you could change tonight. It is still not in the actions table, for a reason you can check: take the 111 channels that published above and below their own median tag count and compare each against itself, and the difference is 0.04 points (0.16 lower to 0.29 higher). Between channels tag count travels with a real difference; inside one channel it does not show up. If you add tags anyway, know this: this file describes two groups as they were on their first trending video. It does not test what happens to your next upload when you change its tags, and nothing in it can.
  • Description presence. 99.56% against 94.76%. Solid, but the entire remaining headroom on the one-hit side is 49 videos.
  • Comments left on. 98.89% against 96.79%. Headroom, 30 videos. Same ceiling.
  • Category mix. Separates hardest, and in both directions — repeat channels more concentrated in Howto & Style, Entertainment, News & Politics and Education, less in People & Blogs by 6.36 points. Wide ranges, and on any one we cannot tell you where in the range you are. No category to tilt toward, and this study does not tell you to change category anyway.
  • Publish slot. Its only separating level is publishing outside both blocks: repeat channels put 5.8 points more of their first videos there (0.2 to 11.2), and we cannot tell you where in that range you are. A slot this study never priced — and inside a single channel the slot difference all but disappears.

Four of the five were checked at once, and when several things are checked together one can look different by luck alone. Allow for that and two hold up, the category mix and the description; the other two travel as the weaker pair rather than being promoted. Either way the shape is the same: they describe the two groups, and describing is not a switch you can flip.

Which way the arrow runs cannot be settled here. The first-video design rules out the circular reading — that repeating created the gap — because the measurement happens before any repeating. It does not rule out the reverse: a channel that already had an audience may both engage more from its first appearance and have the standing to appear again. Settling that needs a subscriber count or a pre-trending baseline, and neither exists in this data at any framing. A live limit, not a footnote.

Nor can cadence be scored: a one-hit channel has no second trending video, so there is no gap between appearances to compare it on — the quantity does not exist rather than being equal. Among repeat channels themselves the typical gap is 18 days, middle half 8.5 to 39.

The answer

The difference is systematic, and it sits in the channel rather than the upload

  • The difference is real and systematic. The two groups differ from their first appearance by about 2,135 reactions on a typical upload — visible before any repeating happened, and surviving all sixteen cuts this file could run. That is the part of "it's all luck" this study can answer, and the answer is no.
  • It is not the settings you decide on each upload, which between them account for about 161 reactions of a 9,810-reaction spread.
  • It is not category, because holding the category mix common across the two groups leaves 74.4% of the gap standing.
  • What is left travels with the channel rather than the upload, and this file has no subscriber count, watch time or click-through to look inside that.

So the difference is real and it is not a to-do list, and the useful consequence is a subtraction: the packaging work you can stop doing, and two rules to apply to anything anyone offers you next.

Appendix

What this is measured on, and what it cannot say
  • The board, not the platform. One row is one video on one trending day: 40,949 rows, 6,351 videos, 2017-11-14 to 2018-06-14. 205 of 213 calendar days are present; eight are missing and both ends of the window are partial. 50 duplicate rows were removed (0.122%) and nothing was imputed.
  • One row per video, at its first day on the board. 101 videos that took more than 30 days to reach the board leave the population, which is what makes "first day" mean the same thing for everyone. 6,250 videos remain. The oldest video at measurement is 28 days old, the typical one 2 days.
  • Smaller channels means channels whose median trending video draws under 354,264 views: 3,489 of 6,250 videos (55.8%) and 1,385 of 2,099 channels (66.0%). Both counts travel with the label. This is most of the board, not a minority of it.
  • Engagement rate is likes plus comments per hundred views, taken video by video and reported as the middle value, not pooled, because pooling hands the answer to the few videos with millions of views. 64 smaller-channel videos have comments or ratings switched off and carry no rate at all; they are reported on their own line rather than folded in.
  • Two numbers matter here: the engagement rate, which is the signal a sponsor reads, and views per upload, which is what engagement gets traded against.
  • What this cannot say. Every video in this file already reached the trending board, so nothing here says whether a video will trend, only how videos that trend differ. There is no subscriber count, no watch time, no click-through and no video duration in the data, so any claim needing one of those is out of scope rather than estimated. US only, one seven-month window.
  • One planned exhibit was not produced. The plan listed a day × hour heatmap among the things carried over from the original brief. It was not built and no finding here rests on it. The publish-slot question was answered on the afternoon and evening blocks instead, and the day-of-week check that would have sat beside a heatmap is reported in the calendar section.
How the numbers were made
  • Basis. One row per video at its first trending snapshot, back-catalogue excluded (videos taking more than 30 days to reach the board: 101 videos, 1.59%). That exclusion moves the all-videos anchor from 3.6732% to 3.7331% and drops the oldest video at measurement from 4,215 days to 28.
  • Middle values, not averages. Engagement rate is right-skewed and views extreme. The top 1% of videos hold 19.45% of all views, so an average would report the giants. Medians of per-video rates throughout; pooled sums are barred for the same reason.
  • Intervals account for channels. Videos from one channel are not independent draws: the intra-channel correlation on ranked engagement is 0.78, so every interval outside the repeat-channel comparison is a channel-clustered bootstrap. A naive video-level interval understates the width by roughly three times. The repeat-channel comparison is exempt by construction: one video per channel on both sides, 450 of 450 first videos verified as strictly earliest with zero ties. The exemption is verified, not assumed.
  • Two method switches, both forced by a check run before the method. One-way ANOVA was rejected for the category ranking (non-normal, variances 8.2 times apart) in favour of Kruskal-Wallis on ranks with bootstrap intervals. The comments-on setting has no engagement rate to explain, because a comments-disabled video has no comment count, so it was moved onto the like-to-dislike ratio instead.
  • Multiplicity, one policy across the study. Detection follows the interval rule declared before computing; Benjamini-Hochberg correction is reported alongside and never promotes a finding. Where a detection does not clear the correction, its confidence is capped at low with that as the stated reason. That is the case for the publish-slot and comments-on descriptors in the repeat-channel comparison, and for both detected calendar gaps.
  • Effect size on the headline gap. Cliff's delta 0.225, from 0.164 to 0.288, a shift between two heavily overlapping distributions rather than two separated populations.
  • Reach convention. A plus means more views and a minus means fewer, and the direction is written in words as well, so nothing depends on reading a sign.
  • Typeface. Set in DM Sans, vendored with the project at weights 300–600. The inherited house style calls four faces from a font CDN; that is a network dependency, and the six charts were already rendered in DM Sans, so matching them keeps a chart and its interactive twin identical offline. No italic face is vendored, so emphasis is carried by weight and colour.
Limits: what would move these numbers
  • One window, one country. 205 of 213 days between 2017-11-14 and 2018-06-14, US only. Eight calendar days are missing (3.76% of the span) and both ends of the window are partial.
  • Columns that do not exist. No subscriber count, watch time, click-through rate or video duration. Every question needing one was reframed or dropped rather than estimated, including the direction of the repeat-channel association, which is the single most consequential thing this file cannot settle.
  • Selection is not even. The comments-and-ratings-off exclusion is roughly tenfold category-skewed (News & Politics 6.37% against Music 0.63%), and 58.7% of all excluded videos sit on smaller channels, the reader's own scale.
  • The detected calendar pair is finely balanced. People & Blogs enters on a lower bound of 0.075 points and accounts for 290 videos, 8.31% of the group; without it the detected set is Entertainment alone, 776 videos, 22.24%. How that behaves under a different random draw was not measured, and is reported as unmeasured rather than guessed.
  • Two aggregate figures are barred outright. The all-categories-together timing figure is a composition artifact, because the two publish blocks do not draw from the same mix of categories, and the everything-versus-everything repeat-channel comparison is circular. Neither is quoted with a number anywhere above.
  • Channel names are not stable keys. 2,207 distinct channel strings appear across the file against 2,099 channels in the ruled population, and 12 videos carry a mid-run channel rename.
  • Trending runs are censored at both edges, and 4.61% of smaller-channel videos touch one. Four videos were removed or errored at some point and are kept, because a removed video still trended.
Off to one side of the argument
  • Categories that hold the board longest are also the categories that engage best. The two orders line up at 0.70 out of a possible 1 across the fourteen rankable categories. It is a live alternative reading of the category ranking, and it rests on fourteen aggregate points and on heavily tied run lengths.
  • Like ratio. 96.88% of ratings are likes on smaller channels, against 97.11% across all trending videos, a second reading of the same reaction rather than a separate decision.
  • Trending run length. Five days for the typical smaller-channel video, and it does not differ by any setting in this file: 5.0 days in both publish blocks, 5.0 tagged and untagged. Censored at both window edges.
  • Tag count is reported in the repeat-channel section above, with the within-channel comparison beside it and the reason it is not in the actions table stated there rather than here.
  • How often repeat channels return. 18 days between trending videos, middle half 8.5 to 39 days, across all 450 repeat channels. It cannot be compared against one-hit channels, who have no second appearance to measure. Though for what it is worth, a one-hit channel's first video sits a median 156 days from the end of the window, so most of them had room to return and did not.
